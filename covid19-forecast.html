LAGS = [1, 2, 3, 7, 14]

def lag_frame(s):
    d = pd.DataFrame({"y": s})
    for k in LAGS:
        d[f"lag{k}"] = s.shift(k)
    d["roll7"] = s.shift(1).rolling(7).mean()
    d["dow"] = s.index.dayofweek
    return d.dropna()

def lgbm_forecast(train_s, horizon=14):
    d = lag_frame(train_s)
    model = LGBMRegressor(n_estimators=300, learning_rate=0.05, num_leaves=15,
                          random_state=42, verbose=-1)
    model.fit(d.drop(columns="y"), d["y"])
    hist, out = train_s.copy(), []
    for _ in range(horizon):
        nxt = hist.index[-1] + pd.Timedelta(days=1)
        row = {f"lag{k}": hist.iloc[-k] for k in LAGS}
        row["roll7"] = hist.iloc[-7:].mean()
        row["dow"] = nxt.dayofweek
        yhat = model.predict(pd.DataFrame([row]))[0]
        hist.loc[nxt] = yhat
        out.append(yhat)
    return np.array(out)

preds["LightGBM (lags)"] = score("LightGBM (lags)", lgbm_forecast(train, HORIZON))

def backtest(fn, series, horizon=14, origins=6, step=7):
    maes = []
    for i in range(origins):
        end = len(series) - horizon - i * step
        tr, te = series.iloc[:end], series.iloc[end:end + horizon]
        p = np.asarray(fn(tr, horizon), dtype=float)
        maes.append(mean_absolute_error(te, p))
    return np.mean(maes), np.std(maes)

models = {
    "Naive": lambda tr, h: np.repeat(tr.iloc[-1], h),
    "7-day moving average": lambda tr, h: np.repeat(tr.iloc[-7:].mean(), h),
    "Holt-Winters": lambda tr, h: ExponentialSmoothing(
        tr, trend="add", damped_trend=True, seasonal="add",
        seasonal_periods=7).fit().forecast(h),
    "SARIMA": lambda tr, h: SARIMAX(
        tr, order=(1,1,1), seasonal_order=(1,0,1,7)).fit(disp=False).forecast(h),
    "LightGBM (lags)": lgbm_forecast,
}

Recommendation for production¶

SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,0,1,7), refit regularly on the latest data.

Reasons:

  • Lowest average error across six rolling 14-day windows (MAE 5,865), roughly 6 to 7% of India's daily cases in September 2020.
  • The seasonal term captures the weekly reporting cycle, and differencing handles the trend.
  • The naive model won the single test window but ranked third on average with the highest variability, so its first win was not reliable.
  • Fast to fit and easy to refresh daily.

Why the others fell short:

  • Holt-Winters extrapolated the trend too aggressively in the final window.
  • LightGBM on lag features cannot predict values above the training range, which is a poor fit for a series still setting new highs.
  • Deep learning was not attempted because 244 days of data is too short.

Limitations: the data covers only the first wave (Jan to Sep 2020); the backtest windows overlap; SARIMA's optimiser did not fully converge in some refits; and forecasts are conditional on no change in testing, lockdown or reporting behaviour.

Task 3: Suggestions for the Government Health Department (India)¶

Based on data to 20 Sep 2020 and the 14-day SARIMA forecast (21 Sep to 4 Oct):

  1. Plan for a sustained plateau of about 90k to 100k new cases per day through early October, not a decline. The forecast total is about 1.3 million cases in 14 days, and the upper end of the 80% interval is about 104k per day. Beds, testing and tracing capacity should be sized for the upper end.
  2. Expect deaths to stay high even if cases level off. Deaths rose after cases in India's data, and the model forecasts about 1,130 to 1,280 deaths per day (about 17,200 over 14 days). Prioritise ICU beds, oxygen supply and ventilators, and protect high-risk groups.
  3. Make decisions on 7-day averages, not single days. Cases fall every Monday (about 19.1k on average versus about 23.9k on Saturdays) because of reporting rhythm, and there are one-day backlog spikes, such as the mid-June deaths jump. Reacting to a single day would send misleading signals.
  4. Keep testing high and publish test positivity. Reported case counts depend on how many tests are done, and deaths as a share of confirmed cases differ widely between countries (India 1.60% versus about 3% for the US and Brazil), partly for that reason. Case counts alone should not be read as a measure of true spread.
  5. Forecast at state level. This model uses national totals, which can hide regional differences. State-level data would let resources be sent where they are needed.
  6. Refresh the model weekly and monitor it. Refit SARIMA on the latest data, and treat actuals falling outside the prediction interval as an early signal that the trend has changed (a peak, a new surge or a change in reporting).

Limitations: only the first 8 months of data are used; forecasts are conditional on no change in testing, restrictions or reporting; and 14 days is the reliable horizon, with longer forecasts becoming much less certain.

# Challenge Technique used Reason
1 Data in wide format with cumulative totals and some countries split by province Summed provinces per country, converted column names to datetimes and took daily differences Forecasting daily new cases needs a country-level daily series, not cumulative province-level counts
2 Negative daily cases/deaths (45 and 55 values) caused by downward revisions of cumulative totals Identified them, and will correct (for example clip to zero or smooth) before modelling A negative number of new cases is impossible, and these values would distort the model and error metrics
3 Weekly reporting zigzag in daily counts Use 7-day rolling averages for analysis; to be handled in modelling (weekly seasonality or smoothed target) Raw daily values mix real trend with reporting patterns
4 One-day outlier in India's daily deaths (2,003 on 16 Jun), likely a reporting backlog Replaced with an interpolated value, refit, and compared forecasts before and after A single backlog day is not real daily deaths; the check showed the forecast total changed by under 2%, so the model was robust to it
5 Final day of data was an incomplete report (daily cases and deaths = 0 for India on 21 Sep) Detected by comparing cumulative totals of the last two days; removed the last day Keeping it would teach the model a false collapse and make the test set unfair
6 Time-ordered data needs a different split from the earlier projects Held out the final 14 days as the test set and trained only on earlier days A random split would leak future information into training and overstate accuracy
7 A baseline beat the trend-based model on the test window, and a single window is unreliable for choosing a model Added a rolling-origin backtest (six 14-day windows) and compare average error Averaging over several windows reduces the influence of luck in one window
8 Only 244 days of data (first wave, no peak for India) Used statistical and lag-feature models; ruled out deep learning (LSTM) A neural network would overfit such a short series
9 LightGBM with lag features failed on a trending series (worst model, MAE about 15k) Compared against statistical models and explained the cause: tree models cannot predict above the training range Shows the model choice must match the data structure, not just the model's reputation
10 SARIMA optimiser convergence warnings during the backtest Noted as a limitation; results remained stable across windows The series is short and trending, so the optimiser does not always fully settle, but the errors were still consistent
11 SARIMA prediction intervals are narrower than the backtest errors suggest Reported the interval with a note that real uncertainty is larger, and used the backtest error as the main accuracy measure Model-based intervals assume the model is correct, which is not guaranteed on a short, trending series

Conclusion¶

  • Data: JHU global time series (188 countries, 22 Jan to 21 Sep 2020), reshaped from cumulative province-level counts into daily country-level series. Negative revisions, an incomplete final day and a deaths backlog spike were corrected.
  • Findings: the US, India and Brazil held about half of global cases. India's new cases were still rising in September (about 93k a day on a 7-day average), unlike the US, Brazil and Colombia, which had already peaked. Daily counts show a weekly reporting cycle, and deaths lag cases.
  • Best model: SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,0,1,7), with a rolling-backtest MAE of about 5.9k on daily cases (versus 7.0k for Holt-Winters, 9.6k for naive and 14.8k for LightGBM). Averaging over six windows changed the ranking from the single test window, where a naive forecast looked best by luck.
  • Forecast (21 Sep to 4 Oct 2020): a plateau of about 90k to 100k cases and about 1,200 deaths per day.
  • Recommendations: plan capacity for the upper bound, use 7-day averages, keep testing high, forecast at state level and refit weekly.
  • Limitations and next steps: only the first wave is covered, the data is national, and the forecast assumes no change in testing or restrictions. Next steps are state-level models, a longer history and adding mobility or testing data.
